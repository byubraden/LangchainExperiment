# Desert Survival: Colorado Plateau and Moab Region

## The Rule of Threes in Desert Context
In canyon country, the order of priorities shifts slightly from general wilderness survival:
- **3 minutes** without air or in flash flood water
- **3 hours** without shade in extreme desert heat (temperatures exceed 100°F July–August in Moab)
- **3 days** without water — but in summer desert heat, severe dehydration can incapacitate in hours
- **3 weeks** without food

**Heat is the primary killer.** Most search and rescue incidents in Arches and Canyonlands involve heat exhaustion or dehydration, not injury.

## Heat Survival
Avoid hiking between 10 AM and 4 PM in summer. Seek shade — canyon walls, overhangs, and rock fins provide relief. Signs of heat exhaustion: dizziness, weakness, nausea, cessation of sweating. Move to shade immediately, elevate legs, and hydrate slowly with water and electrolytes. Heat stroke (confusion, loss of consciousness, hot dry skin) is a medical emergency — cool the victim rapidly and activate emergency services.

Flash floods occur suddenly, even in clear skies, when rain falls miles away in the watershed. Never camp in a wash. If water begins rising or you hear a roaring sound upstream, immediately move to high ground — do not try to outrun moving water.

## Water in the Desert
Water sources in canyon country are scarce and often unreliable. Cryptobiotic seeps and potholes may be the only sources for miles. Do not contaminate potholes — they support entire micro-ecosystems and may be the only water source for wildlife in the area.

Purification: all backcountry water must be treated. Boiling (1 minute), a hollow-fiber filter (Sawyer, Katadyn), or chemical tablets (Aquatabs) are all acceptable. UV purification (SteriPen) works but requires batteries and clear water.

Carry 4+ liters per person for any backcountry day in summer.

## Navigation and Getting Found
Cell service is unreliable in most of the canyon country around Moab. Before any backcountry trip:
- Download offline topo maps (Gaia GPS, CalTopo, or the NPS app)
- Carry a physical topo map and compass
- File a trip plan with someone who will call for help if you don't return
- Consider a satellite communicator (Garmin inReach) for remote trips

If lost: stay put. Searchers will converge on your last known location. Use a signal mirror in open canyon country — visible for miles. Three whistle blasts is the universal distress signal.

## Flash Flood Protocol
- Monitor weather not just overhead but across the entire watershed upstream
- "Turn around, don't drown" — 6 inches of moving water can knock a person down; 12 inches can sweep a vehicle
- If caught in a slot canyon during a flood, climb the walls as high as possible and hold on
- Never camp in washes, drainages, or canyon floors without checking the forecast
